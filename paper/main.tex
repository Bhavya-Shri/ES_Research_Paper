\begin{abstract}
Conventional personal-audio limiters reduce listening level by applying one gain to every frequency. A-weighted exposure and programme loudness do not weight the spectrum in the same way, so where the cut is spent can change which meter wins. This paper reports a matched test of three digital systems on 20 clips (16 Freesound recordings and four synthetic signals): frequency-flat gain (S0); a bass-first three-band heuristic with a $-12$\,dB low shelf, harmonic fill, and a mild mid compressor (S1); and the same processor with the shelf and harmonics off and a stronger mid cut (S2). At matched BS.1770 loudness (LUFS), S1's digital A-weighted $L_{Aeq}$ proxy is on average 1.17\,dB \emph{higher} than S0. The bass-first hypothesis is not supported. S2 reverses the sign: $-0.27$\,dB versus S0 (bootstrap 95\% CI $[-0.45,\,-0.11]$). The margin is modest. The same pairs scored with a C-weighted proxy flip again: S1 looks better on $L_{Ceq}$ ($-0.58$\,dB) and S2 slightly worse ($+0.06$\,dB). Frequency-selective limiting is not uniformly better or worse than flat gain; it is better for the meter whose weight is large where the processor cuts. All levels are digital-domain proxies, not calibrated ear-level sound pressure. S2 is a configuration of S1, not a new limiter.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Personal listening devices can deliver high playback levels for long periods. The World Health Organization and ITU-T Recommendation H.870 treat this as a cumulative A-weighted sound-dose problem and give device makers a framework for tracking exposure \cite{H870,WHO2021,Levey2011,Portnuff2011}. H.870 specifies \emph{how much} dose to allow. It does not pick a signal-processing algorithm. Device algorithms nonetheless exist: broadband dynamic-range control for H.870 listening \cite{Chen2023}, time-weighted-average (TWA) limiting with optional multiband companding \cite{US9980028}, and headset dosimetry that applies frequency-flat gain \cite{US6826515}. The standard is silent on allocation; that is not the same as ``no algorithm exists.''

The usual engineering response is a broadband limiter or automatic gain control \cite{Giannoulis2012}: when an exposure estimate exceeds a threshold, apply one scalar $G<1$ to the whole signal. That is easy to implement and it does reduce an A-weighted metric. It is also crude. Equal-loudness contours \cite{FletcherMunson1933,ISO226} show that hearing sensitivity is strongly frequency-dependent, so a flat cut changes bass, midrange, and treble by different perceptual amounts. Multiband compression and virtual-bass methods already exist as separate tools. What is missing is a direct test of \emph{where} to spend attenuation when the constraint is A-weighted dose and the comparison is programme loudness.

That is the question of this paper. We ask:

\begin{quote}
\textit{At matched loudness, can frequency-selective gain reduce a digital A-weighted exposure proxy more than turning everything down, and must that cut follow $w_A(f)$ rather than a bass-first heuristic?}
\end{quote}

We compare three systems on the same audio: frequency-flat gain (S0), a bass-first three-band heuristic (S1), and a metric-aligned configuration of the same processor (S2). We then match either LUFS or the digital $L_{Aeq}$ proxy and compare the other quantity. The ingredients are not new. The comparison is the contribution.

Three findings follow. First, at matched LUFS, S1 has a \emph{higher} digital $L_{Aeq}$ proxy than S0, by 1.17\,dB on average over 20 clips. Bass-heavy programme accounts for almost all of the difference. Second, S2---no low shelf, no harmonics, stronger mid-band cut---reverses that sign ($-0.27$\,dB). The interval excludes zero; the margin is modest and is not a large dose saving. Third, scoring the same pairs with C-weighting flips both conclusions, so the result is meter-dependence, not a universal limiter. Section~\ref{sec:results} shows the mechanism: A-weighting already ignores energy near 40\,Hz, while BS.1770 K-weighting does not.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related methods and states the gap. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes S1 and S2 as design heuristics, not as a numerical solver. Section~\ref{sec:impl} records implementation and verification. Section~\ref{sec:setup} defines the experiment. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the 20-clip comparison. Section~\ref{sec:limits} collects limitations in one place. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Exposure control}

H.870 defines an A-weighted sound-allowance model and discusses the difficulty of mapping a digital signal to ear-level pressure, which depends on transducer sensitivity and volume setting \cite{H870}. Device-side responses are typically volume caps, dose meters, or broadband automatic gain reduction \cite{Levey2011,Sulaiman2013,Chen2023}. Chen \emph{et al.} combine H.870 dose tracking, speaker frequency-response correction, and broadband DRC to lower estimated playback level as allowance is used \cite{Chen2023}. US~6\,826\,515 is a headset dosimeter whose corrective gain is frequency-flat, so the chosen equalisation is preserved \cite{US6826515}; that is S0 in patent form. US~9\,980\,028 is a TWA limiter that may use multiband companding to keep speech loudness while cutting RMS energy \cite{US9980028}. AES technical document TD1008 recommends LUFS for streaming distribution and notes that portable players remain an H.870 / EN~50332-3 dose problem \cite{AESTD1008}; it does not specify a frequency-allocation algorithm. Those systems manage level, or allocate it for speech-headset intelligibility. They do not report a matched LUFS versus digital $L_{Aeq}$ comparison on music.

\subsection{Dynamic range control}

Digital compressors and limiters are well understood \cite{Giannoulis2012,Kates2008}. Multiband designs apply a separate envelope in each band and are usually tuned for tonal balance, broadcast loudness, or hearing-aid audibility---not for an exposure-versus-loudness comparison against a flat baseline. Multi-band hearing-protection processors \cite{Fathima2026,US11006215} allocate gain across frequency to tame transients or keep an occupational level limit; they are adjacent, but they are not a matched LUFS/$L_{Aeq}$ test on music. ITU-R BS.1770 is the standard programme-loudness meter used here as a loudness stand-in \cite{BS1770}. IEC~61672 also defines C-weighting, which keeps much more low-frequency energy than A \cite{IEC61672}; concert and occupational arguments often prefer C. We use C only as a second digital proxy, not as a calibrated workplace meter.

\subsection{Loudness perception}

ISO~226 equal-loudness contours \cite{ISO226} and excitation-pattern models \cite{Zwicker1961,ZwickerFastl1999,MooreGlasbergBaer1997,GlasbergMoore2002} explain why a flat gain change is not perceptually flat. This paper uses that fact as motivation. It does not implement a Zwicker or Moore--Glasberg loudness model. Integrated LUFS is the operational loudness measure.

\subsection{Virtual bass}

When a harmonic complex loses its fundamental, listeners can still hear a pitch at $f_0$ \cite{Schouten1940,Terhardt1974,Terhardt1979}. Loudspeaker virtual-bass systems exploit that residue pitch by synthesising harmonics when the driver cannot reproduce sub-bass \cite{BenTzur1999,Gan2001,Gan2004}. S1 uses the same mechanism in the opposite direction: attenuate physical sub-bass and add harmonics in 80--400\,Hz. Residue pitch is not the same thing as preserved bass loudness, and we do not treat it as such. S2 turns this path off.

What is scarce is a matched LUFS versus digital $L_{Aeq}$ test of a bass-first heuristic \emph{and} a $w_A$-aligned configuration against flat gain on music. That comparison is what this study provides. It does not introduce a new limiter architecture.

\section{Proposed Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Design objective}

Let $x[n]$ be the input. A conventional limiter applies one gain
\begin{equation}
y[n] = G\, x[n], \qquad G < 1.
\label{eq:flat}
\end{equation}
Call that rule \textbf{S0}. We want a frequency-dependent alternative that, for a given loudness, yields a lower A-weighted digital exposure proxy than \eqref{eq:flat}. Preferentially cutting energy that contributes more to A-weighting than to loudness is the intended trade-off. Preferentially cutting energy that A-weighting already ignores is the failure mode.

This is a design objective, not a solved optimisation. We do not run a numerical solver for a constrained cost. Both S1 and S2 are fixed three-band heuristics on the same code path.

\subsection{Weighting mismatch}

Fig.~\ref{fig:wak} plots IEC A-weighting and BS.1770 K-weighting, and their ratio $W_A/W_K$, from 20\,Hz to 20\,kHz. Near 40\,Hz, A-weighting is about $-35$\,dB while digital K-weighting is only a few dB down. A cut there spends loudness (LUFS sees K) that an A-weighted dose meter barely records. Near 1--4\,kHz the two weights are closer, and A is large, so a mid cut should move $L_{Aeq}$ more than a bass cut of the same loudness cost. That ratio is the design rule. S1 ignores it; S2 follows it.

\subsection{Architecture}

The signal is split at 300\,Hz and 4\,kHz, processed per band, summed, and passed through a fixed-ceiling peak limiter (Fig.~\ref{fig:block}):
\begin{equation}
y[n] = y_{\mathrm{L}}[n] + y_{\mathrm{M}}[n] + y_{\mathrm{H}}[n].
\end{equation}
The low band carries sub-bass, the mid band most speech and musical energy, and the high band many transients. Those cutoffs were chosen for the prototype. They were not searched over listeners.

\subsection{Low band}

\textbf{S1} applies a low shelf of $\beta = 10^{-12/20} \approx 0.25$ ($-12$\,dB) around 100\,Hz. Harmonics are generated from the isolated sub-bass $x_{\mathrm{sub}}$ by a memoryless nonlinearity
\begin{equation}
x_{\mathrm{NLD}}[n] = a_2\, x_{\mathrm{sub}}^2[n] + a_3\, x_{\mathrm{sub}}^3[n]
\end{equation}
with $a_2=0.8$ and $a_3=0.35$, then band-pass filtered to 80--400\,Hz and mixed back. An earlier 160--300\,Hz harmonic window missed $2f_0$ and $3f_0$ for fundamentals near 40\,Hz. The wider window is used so that a 40\,Hz tone actually produces energy at 80 and 120\,Hz (Fig.~\ref{fig:harm}). Harmonic injection supports a residue pitch cue. It is not evidence that bass loudness is preserved.

\textbf{S2} sets the shelf to $0$\,dB and the harmonic mix to $0$. The low path is then the complementary crossover output, unaltered.

\subsection{Mid band}

The mid band is compressed from an A-weighted envelope. If $e[n]$ is that envelope and
\begin{equation}
L[n] = 20\log_{10}(e[n]+\varepsilon) + L_{\mathrm{ref}},
\end{equation}
gain reduction of ratio $R=4$ is applied only when $L[n]$ exceeds a target (the same uncalibrated digital offset $L_{\mathrm{ref}}=94$ used in the exposure proxy):
\begin{equation}
g_{\mathrm{M}}[n] = 10^{-(1-1/R)\max(L[n]-L_{\mathrm{tgt}},0)/20}.
\end{equation}
Attack and release are 5\,ms and 50\,ms. S1 uses $L_{\mathrm{tgt}}=78$; S2 uses $L_{\mathrm{tgt}}=72$. The S2 target was frozen before the 20-clip mean was inspected; it was not retuned to enlarge $\Delta L_{Aeq}$. The A-weighting filter is the IEC~61672 analog prototype \cite{IEC61672}, bilinearly transformed at 48\,kHz and normalised to 0\,dB at 1\,kHz. Inside 300\,Hz--4\,kHz, where this detector is used, the digital magnitude tracks the analytic curve to well under 0.2\,dB. Above about 8\,kHz the bilinear transform warps the response; that region is not used for mid-band detection (Fig.~\ref{fig:aw}).

\subsection{High band}

Content above 4\,kHz is scaled by a one-pole envelope $E[n]$:
\begin{equation}
y_{\mathrm{H}}[n] = x_{\mathrm{H}}[n]\cdot \frac{1}{1+\gamma \max(0, E[n]-T_{\mathrm{H}})},
\end{equation}
with $\gamma=0.5$ and $T_{\mathrm{H}}=-12$\,dBFS. This is transient-aware gain control. It is not a validated perceptual model. S1 and S2 share this path.

\subsection{Crossover reconstruction}

Independent Butterworth low/band/high splits are not magnitude-complementary and can boost the sum near the cutoffs. The processor uses cascaded fourth-order Linkwitz--Riley sections \cite{Linkwitz1976}, with allpass compensation of the low path at the upper split, so the three bands sum to an allpass. Fig.~\ref{fig:xo} compares reconstruction with the older independent-Butterworth bank: the Linkwitz--Riley sum sits at 0\,dB, while the older bank peaked at the 300\,Hz and 4\,kHz boundaries and raised a quiet 440\,Hz tone by 19.7\%.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}

The reference system is offline Python (\texttt{numpy}/\texttt{scipy}) at $f_s=48$\,kHz. All filters are causal: second-order sections with state carried across blocks. The processor is hop-based (256 samples) and streamable. Processing a 2\,s test signal in 240-sample blocks versus in one call gave a maximum absolute sample difference of 0.0. The output stage is a peak ceiling at $-1$\,dBFS; it never renormalises a quiet block back toward full scale.

S1 and S2 share that code path. S2 is \texttt{ProposedConfig.aligned()}: shelf $0$\,dB, harmonic mix $0$, mid target $72$. Parameters are listed in Table~\ref{tab:params}. Source code is the \texttt{exposure\_dsp} package in the project repository. Listings are omitted from the paper.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Systems}

Every clip is processed by four labelled outputs:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Original}: no processing.
\item \textbf{S0}, frequency-flat gain: one scalar $G$ applied to the whole waveform. This is the baseline that answers ``turn everything down.'' Each of S1 and S2 has its own S0 match.
\item \textbf{S1}: the three-band processor at the S1 column of Table~\ref{tab:params}.
\item \textbf{S2}: the same processor at the S2 column.
\end{enumerate}

A secondary A-weighted broadband compressor was also matched by searching its threshold. On many clips it never engaged, because the uncalibrated proxy never crossed the default target. Idle compression is not a fair ``turn it down'' baseline, so the reported comparison uses static flat gain.

\subsection{Matching}

Two matching rules are applied to each of S1 and S2, each to within a few hundredths of a dB:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Matched exposure:} $G$ is chosen so the S0 output has the same digital $L_{Aeq}$ proxy as the processor.
\item \textbf{Matched loudness:} $G$ is chosen so the S0 output has the same integrated LUFS \cite{BS1770} as the processor (\texttt{pyloudnorm}).
\end{itemize}

Because flat gain is frequency-independent, both meters move by approximately $20\log_{10} G$. The two matching rules are therefore two views of one difference, not two independent experiments. The interesting content is the sign of that difference and which material produces it.

\subsection{Metrics}

The digital exposure proxy is A-weighted RMS plus an arbitrary offset,
\begin{equation}
L_{Aeq}^{\mathrm{proxy}} = 20\log_{10}\mathrm{RMS}\{w_A * x\} + 94.
\end{equation}
A C-weighted proxy $L_{Ceq}^{\mathrm{proxy}}$ uses the IEC~61672 C analog prototype in place of $w_A$, with the same offset \cite{IEC61672}. The offset 94 maps digital full scale to a convenient number for development. It is \emph{not} a calibration to pascals or to an ear simulator. Loudness is integrated LUFS. Spectral change versus the original is log-spectral distance (LSD) over Welch power estimates; LSD is an objective distance, not a listening-test score.

\subsection{Material}

The corpus has $n=20$ clips at 48\,kHz mono: 16 Creative Commons Freesound recordings (bass loops, drum loops, choir, solo vocal, speech, cymbals)\footnote{Freesound IDs 165523, 179270, 189635, 269906, 380303, 431525, 479941, 529808, 541260, 627643, 628213, 629137, 629139, 657761, 735157, 739037.} and four built-in synthetic programmes (bass-heavy, speech-like, transient, mixed). Synthetics are included for completeness and labelled as such. The conclusions below are also stated for the 16 real recordings alone.

\subsection{Hypotheses}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{H2-S1:} at equal LUFS, S1's $L_{Aeq}$ proxy is lower than S0.
\item \textbf{H2-S2:} at equal LUFS, S2's $L_{Aeq}$ proxy is lower than S0.
\item \textbf{H1-S1:} at equal $L_{Aeq}$ proxy, S1 is louder (higher LUFS) than S0.
\item \textbf{HC:} at equal LUFS, S1 $\Delta L_{Ceq}$ is lower (better) than S1 $\Delta L_{Aeq}$, and S2 does the opposite.
\end{itemize}

H2-S1 and H1-S1 are the original bass-first claims. H2-S2 and HC were written before the C-weighted means were inspected. S1 and S2 knobs were not retuned after seeing $\Delta$.

\subsection{Listening notes}

No formal MUSHRA or PEAQ test was run, and no listener scores are reported. A later test should lock playback volume, randomise order, and collect ratings for overall impression, bass, speech, and loudness. Until that is done, no statement in this paper is a listening-test result. S2's mid cut in particular may be audible on speech; LSD does not settle that question.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{S1 at matched loudness}

Fig.~\ref{fig:h2} plots, for each clip, S1's digital $L_{Aeq}$ proxy minus S0 at matched LUFS. Positive bars mean S1 is \emph{worse} on the quantity it was supposed to reduce.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{matched_loudness_delta.png}
\caption{Digital $L_{Aeq}$ proxy at matched LUFS: S1 minus frequency-flat gain. Positive values mean S1 has the higher exposure proxy. Bass loops (red) drive the mean; speech, vocals, and short transients sit near zero.}
\label{fig:h2}
\end{figure*}

The mean over 20 clips is $+1.17$\,dB (median $+0.61$\,dB). On the 16 Freesound recordings alone the mean is $+0.92$\,dB. Twelve clips are more than 0.05\,dB worse for S1, six are within 0.05\,dB, and two are slightly better (a female vocal at $-0.07$\,dB and mixed command speech at $-0.12$\,dB). \textbf{H2-S1 is not supported.}

The failure is concentrated in bass. The six real bass and drum-loop clips sit between $+1.59$ and $+2.67$\,dB (mean $+1.98$\,dB). Speech, choir, solo vocal, and cymbal clips as a group average $+0.28$\,dB. Synthetic bass and mixed programmes are more extreme ($+4.91$ and $+3.87$\,dB) because they were built with strong 40--50\,Hz components; they inflate the overall mean but they do not create the finding. Real bass loops already go the same way.

A Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the 20 S1 $\Delta L_{Aeq}$ values rejects a zero median two-sided ($p=3.9\times 10^{-4}$; rank-biserial $r=0.84$). The one-sided test of H2-S1 ($\Delta<0$) is not supported ($p\approx 1$). The bootstrap 95\% CI on the mean is $[0.60,\,1.83]$\,dB. On the 16 real recordings the mean is $+0.92$\,dB (CI $[0.47,\,1.39]$; two-sided $p=0.002$). The six real bass loops sit at $+1.98$\,dB (CI $[1.69,\,2.33]$).

\subsection{Why S1 moves the wrong way}

Fig.~\ref{fig:mismatch} plots, for the S1 output versus the original, LUFS drop against $L_{Aeq}$ drop. If the two meters agreed, points would lie on the dashed line. Bass clips lie far to the right: LUFS falls by 2--5\,dB while the A-weighted proxy barely moves. Speech and cymbals lie near the origin.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{metric_mismatch.png}
\caption{S1 versus the original clip. Bass-heavy material loses loudness (LUFS) without a matching drop in the A-weighted proxy, because A-weighting already ignores energy near 40\,Hz.}
\label{fig:mismatch}
\end{figure}

A-weighting at 40\,Hz is approximately $-35$\,dB (Fig.~\ref{fig:aw}). BS.1770 K-weighting does not discount that region to the same degree (Fig.~\ref{fig:wak}). The low-band shelf therefore does what it was designed to do---remove sub-bass---and LUFS notices. The exposure proxy does not. To match LUFS, S0 must apply that same loudness reduction to the \emph{whole} spectrum, including the midrange that A-weighting does count. Flat $L_{Aeq}$ therefore falls further. Frequency-selective allocation aimed at ``cheap'' bass cuts is the wrong allocation for an A-weighted objective.

This is not a crossover bug. Reconstruction is flat (Fig.~\ref{fig:xo}), harmonics are present at 40\,Hz (Fig.~\ref{fig:harm}), and block streaming matches whole-signal processing. The negative S1 result is a metric mismatch, not an implementation failure.

\subsection{S2 reverses the sign}

Fig.~\ref{fig:s1s2} plots S1$-$S0 and S2$-$S0 on the same clips at matched LUFS. S2 spends attenuation in the mid band, where $w_A$ is large.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{matched_loudness_s1_s2.png}

\label{fig:s1s2}

\subsection{C-weighting dependence}

The same matched-LUFS pairs were re-scored with $L_{Ceq}$ (same RMS-plus-offset helper; not ear-level sound pressure). The hypothesis, written before looking, was HC above. C at 40\,Hz is about $-2$\,dB; A is about $-35$\,dB. On $n=20$, S1 mean $\Delta L_{Aeq}=+1.17$\,dB but mean $\Delta L_{Ceq}=-0.58$\,dB (bootstrap 95\% CI $[-0.94,\,-0.26]$; two-sided Wilcoxon $p=1.0\times 10^{-4}$). S2 mean $\Delta L_{Aeq}=-0.27$\,dB but mean $\Delta L_{Ceq}=+0.06$\,dB (CI $[0.02,\,0.12]$; $p=0.007$). Both sign predictions hold. Frequency-selective limiting is not uniformly better or worse than flat gain; it is better for the meter whose weight is large where the processor cuts. H.870 chose A. These remain digital-domain proxies. \textbf{HC is supported.}

\subsection{S1 at matched exposure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:h1} is the dual view for S1. At equal digital $L_{Aeq}$, S1 is quieter by 1.17\,dB LUFS on average---exactly the opposite sign of Fig.~\ref{fig:h2}, as expected for a frequency-flat baseline. \textbf{H1-S1 is not supported as a loudness-preservation claim.} At the same A-weighted proxy, turning the whole signal down keeps more LUFS than cutting sub-bass.

\subsection{Ablation of S1}

Fig.~\ref{fig:abl} shows mean change versus the original when S1 bands are enabled one at a time. This ablation was not repeated for S2. Crossover-only reconstruction does not change level. Mid-only compression moves $L_{Aeq}$ on speech and vocals and does little on bass. Adding the low band is what drops LUFS. Adding the high band changes almost nothing on this corpus; transients in the test set rarely exceeded the high-band threshold. The full S1 system tracks the mid+low curve. The exposure/loudness disagreement is produced by the low shelf, which is also the block that was supposed to be the advantage.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

This study compares digital processing rules. It does not show prevention of hearing loss, and it does not report ear-level SPL. The $L_{Aeq}$ and $L_{Ceq}$ figures use an uncalibrated offset; a real mapping would require a specific earphone, volume setting, and an IEC~60318-4 occluded-ear simulator \cite{IEC60318}, which still is not an individual human ear.

LUFS is a standard loudness meter, not a booth loudness match. Band edges, shelf depth, and compressor constants were not searched over listeners. No Zwicker or Moore--Glasberg model is in the loop. No PEAQ or MUSHRA scores were collected. S2's mid cut may be objectionable on speech; that is untested. The S1 ablation was not repeated for S2. The S2 margin ($-0.27$\,dB) is a sign flip with a confidence interval that excludes zero, not a large operational saving. Four synthetic clips are in the mean; removing them does not reverse S1 or S2 on $L_{Aeq}$.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked whether a three-band heuristic could reduce a digital A-weighted exposure proxy more than turning the whole signal down, at the same LUFS, and whether that requires cutting where $w_A$ is large. On 20 clips the bass-first system (S1) did not: its proxy was 1.17\,dB higher on average, and 1.6 to 2.7\,dB higher on real bass loops. The same processor with the shelf off and a stronger mid cut (S2) reversed the sign ($-0.27$\,dB). C-weighting flipped both conclusions. The useful outcome is the allocation rule, not a new limiter: if an A-weighted dose limit is the constraint, spend attenuation where $w_A(f)$ is large; bass-first cuts are a poor allocation for that meter. The S2 margin is modest. Calibrated ear-simulator measurements and a locked-volume listening test remain the next experimental step.
